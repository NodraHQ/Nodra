01. Introduction

Introduction

Buying your first crypto is less about clicking the right button and more about understanding where you are buying, who holds your money while you do it, and what you can do afterwards. This lesson walks through the main ways to buy and then follows one complete example, step by step: creating an account on an exchange, securing it, adding funds and making a first purchase. Moving what you bought to a wallet you control is covered in a separate self-custody lesson.

The example uses MEXC, a global exchange. It is there to show how the process works, not as a recommendation: every platform has its own menus, rules and availability, and all of them change over time. What stays the same from one platform to another is the logic, and that is what you will learn here.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to tell the main ways of buying crypto apart, explain the difference between a service linked to your country and a global one, follow the full flow from account to first purchase, and compare the ways of adding funds (Pix, card and peer to peer) by their real cost and implications.

02. Ways to Buy

Ways to Buy

There is no single place to buy crypto. In practice, most beginners use one of three paths, and each one trades convenience, cost and risk in a different way.

Exchange

A company runs a marketplace. You deposit money, buy, and the company keeps the asset for you until you withdraw it.

Peer to peer (P2P)

You buy directly from another person. The platform holds the crypto in escrow while you pay the seller, and releases it only when the seller confirms.

Apps and wallets

Some apps let you pay by card or bank transfer inside the app, through a partner. It is the simplest route, and usually the most expensive.

None of them is "the right one". On an exchange, adding funds usually happens through Pix or bank transfer, by card, or by P2P, and each has different costs and implications (covered below). For people who already know the market, P2P tends to add little: the price is rarely better than buying directly, and you take on the risk of dealing with another person. An app is the quickest way to buy a small amount without learning anything else. This lesson focuses on the exchange, because it teaches how the market actually works.

03. Crypto Has No Borders

Crypto Has No Borders

Crypto networks do not belong to any country: anyone with an internet connection can use the same network. The companies that offer services on top of those networks are a different story. Each one is established somewhere, follows the rules of the place where it operates, and chooses which countries it serves.

That creates two broad groups. Services linked to Brazil usually have a company in the country or clearly aim at Brazilians: support in Portuguese, a .br domain, local advertising, local payment methods or partnerships with Brazilian companies. Global services operate from other countries, often have no office here and answer to the rules of where they are established, even when Brazilians use them every day.

Service linked to Brazil

Support and terms in Portuguese, a local company you can contact, and operations handled under Brazilian rules. Information about your operations is reported by the platform itself.

Global service

Rules, support and legal responsibility come from another country. It may accept Brazilians and local payment methods without being a Brazilian company, and it does not report on your behalf in Brazil.

Pix shows why this is not obvious. Pix only means that money moves between Brazilian bank accounts. When you buy in a P2P market, you send Pix to another person, while the platform, which may be based abroad, only holds the crypto during the trade. Paying with Pix does not make a global service local, and a service that accepts Pix is not automatically Brazilian: what counts is who runs it and where.

One practical consequence is reporting. Platforms considered to operate in Brazil report their users' operations to the Receita Federal under the rules in force. A global platform without that link does not do it for you, so the record of your purchases, sales and transfers (dates, amounts and values in reais) is yours to keep, and Brazilian regulation also sets duties for people who use foreign platforms. The current framework is the DeCripto (IN RFB 2.291/2025), which replaces the older IN RFB 1.888/2019. Rules change often, so check the current ones with the Receita Federal or with an accountant who knows crypto.

Key takeaway: before using any platform, find out which company runs it, where it is based, which countries it serves and who answers to you if something goes wrong.

04. How an Exchange Works

How an Exchange Works

An exchange is a marketplace where buyers and sellers meet. When you deposit money, the exchange credits a balance on its own books. That balance is a claim on the company: until you withdraw, the company, not you, controls the private keys of the assets. That is why the lessons on wallets insist on the idea of self custody.

Assets on an exchange are traded in pairs, written like BTC/USDT. In that pair, you buy BTC (the first asset) and pay with USDT (the second). USDT is a stablecoin that tracks the US dollar, which is why many exchanges ask you to get a stablecoin first and then use it to buy other assets.

This lesson covers only spot trading, which means buying and selling the real asset at the current price. Products such as futures and leverage are a different matter, with risks that do not belong in a first purchase.

05. Account, Security and Verification

Account, Security and Verification

The next three sections follow the same example from the start. First, the account itself: creating it, protecting it and verifying your identity. Do these steps in this order, because a protected account is the foundation for everything else.

Step 1. Create the account

On the official site or app, sign up with an e-mail or phone number and a strong password, then confirm the code you receive. Type the address yourself or use a bookmark you created: never trust a link from an ad or a message.

KYC stands for "know your customer". Platforms ask for it to meet regulations and to fight fraud, and most features stay limited until it is done. Send your documents only through the official site or app, and never to someone who contacts you offering to "speed up" the verification.

06. Adding Funds

Adding Funds

To buy, you need a balance on the platform. There are a few common ways to add it, and each has different implications for cost, speed, limits and, as seen in "Crypto Has No Borders", for who is on the other side of the payment. What you see depends on the platform, your country and your account, and it changes over time, so always check inside the app before planning.

Step 4. Add funds

Pix or bank transfer

Many global platforms do not receive Pix themselves. The payment usually goes to a third-party payment provider, which sells you the crypto and credits your account. It is normally cheaper than a card, but the cost often sits in the exchange rate and spread instead of a visible fee. The receipt may show a company that is not the platform, and providers commonly require the paying account to be in the name of the verified holder.

Card

The fastest and most convenient, and usually the most expensive: provider fee plus spread. If the provider is abroad, your issuer can treat it as an international purchase, with extra charges on the statement, and some issuers block or flag crypto purchases. Limits are low at first. Avoid paying in installments or with credit you cannot settle: the asset can lose value while the bill stays the same.

P2P

You pay another person, and the platform holds the crypto in escrow until the seller confirms. For experienced users it adds little, because the price is rarely better than buying directly and you take on counterparty risk. If you use it: pay only inside the order, from an account in your name, never move the conversation to another app, and mark the payment as made only after the money has left your account.

Deposit crypto you already own

If you already hold crypto elsewhere, you can send it to the platform. The network you choose must match the one of the deposit address, otherwise the funds can be lost.

Before choosing, compare:

  • The total cost, not only the displayed fee: compare the rate you get with a reference price at that moment.
  • Limits and time to credit, which are usually lower and slower for new accounts.
  • Who receives your payment, and whether the paying account has to be in your name.
  • The records you keep: receipts, dates and amounts in reais are part of your history, as seen in "Crypto Has No Borders".
07. Buying on the Spot Market

Buying on the Spot Market

Step 5. Buy on the spot market

With a balance in the account (for example, in USDT), you can buy the asset you want on the spot market. Choose the pair, decide the order type, enter an amount, review the fee and confirm.

Market order

Executes immediately at the best price available. Simple and fast, but the final price can be slightly different from the one you saw, especially in assets with little volume.

Limit order

Executes only when the price reaches the value you set. You control the price, but the order may never be filled if the market does not get there.

Fees are the real cost of buying. A platform charges a percentage of each trade (a "maker" fee when your order waits in the book, a "taker" fee when it matches an existing one immediately), and the difference between buy and sell prices (the spread) also works like a cost. As an illustration only: with a 0.1% fee, spending 100 USDT costs about 0.1 USDT in fees. Look at the actual figures shown before you confirm.

08. Safety Checklist

Safety Checklist

Most losses among beginners do not come from the market: they come from mistakes in the process. This list covers the ones that repeat the most.

Fake sites and apps

Paid ads and links in messages often lead to copies of real platforms. Type the address yourself, keep a bookmark and install apps only from the official stores.

Fake support

Real support never asks for your seed phrase, your two-step codes or for you to install remote access software. Anyone who does is a scammer.

Check the platform

Look for the company behind it, where it is based and whether it is allowed to serve people in your country. If it is not, understand that recovering money and getting help can be much harder.

Do not keep everything there

Keep on the exchange only what you are about to trade. Moving the rest to a wallet you control is covered in the self-custody lesson.

Promises of easy returns

Guaranteed returns, "signals" and urgent offers from strangers are the usual bait. Real markets do not guarantee anything.

Keep your records

Save the dates, amounts and values of what you buy, sell and send. Platforms can change or shut down, and your history should not depend on them.

09. Summary

Summary

  • There are three common ways to buy: exchange, P2P and apps with built-in purchase. Each trades convenience, cost and risk differently.
  • Crypto networks have no borders, but the companies offering services do: where they are based and who they serve matters.
  • Paying with Pix does not make a service Brazilian. The payment may go to a third-party provider or to another person.
  • A global platform does not report your operations in Brazil for you, so keeping your own records is part of the process.
  • On an exchange, the balance is a claim on the company. Self custody starts when you move assets to your own wallet, which is the topic of a separate lesson.
  • Secure the account before adding money: strong password, authenticator app and the extra protections offered.
  • Pix, card and P2P differ in cost, limits and who receives the payment. Compare the total cost, not only the displayed fee.
  • Card is usually the most expensive, and should never be paid with credit you cannot settle.
  • Market orders are immediate. Limit orders wait for your price. Fees and spread are part of the cost.
10. Glossary

Glossary

Exchange
A company that runs a marketplace where users buy and sell crypto.
P2P (peer to peer)
A purchase made directly from another person, with the platform holding the crypto in escrow.
Escrow (garantia)
Assets held by a third party until both sides of a trade fulfil their part.
KYC
Know your customer: identity verification required by a platform.
2FA
Two-step verification: a second proof, such as a code from an authenticator app, besides the password.
Trading pair
Two assets traded against each other, such as BTC/USDT.
Spot market
The market where the real asset is bought and sold at the current price.
Market order
An order that executes immediately at the best available price.
Limit order
An order that executes only when the price reaches the value you set.
Spread
The difference between the best buy price and the best sell price.
Maker and taker fees
Percentage charged on trades: maker when your order waits in the book, taker when it matches immediately.
Payment provider
A third-party company that receives your Pix or card payment and delivers the crypto to your account.
Withdrawal whitelist
A list of pre-approved addresses; withdrawals can only go to them.
